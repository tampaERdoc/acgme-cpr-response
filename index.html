Why this matters

The ACGME is proposing a major revision of the Common Program Requirements that apply to every residency. The ACGME presents it as a 43 percent reduction in the number of requirements. For Emergency Medicine the effect is different: in several areas it deletes existing EM-specific standards and removes or narrows the EM Review Committee’s authority to put them back. The proposal goes to the ACGME Board in June 2027 with an effective date of July 1, 2028. The public comment period closes October 22, 2026. Individual comments are counted; a department-level letter is read once.

Three themes run through every answer in this guide. If you write anything in your own words, write to these:

1. StandardsThe ACGME's job is to guarantee that every accredited program produces a highly trained physician for the community it serves. Requirements that are easier to meet are not the same as requirements that are better for patients or residents.
2. Specialty controlThe ACGME should set the floor and let each specialty, through its Review Committee, adapt, modify, and adopt the requirements that fit its field. Emergency Medicine should be able to keep a 60-hour week, a 12-hour shift, a 1:3 core faculty ratio, and EM-trained supervision because those standards fit emergency care.
3. Balance against corporate influenceGME expansion in EM over the last decade has been dominated by programs outside academic institutions. Corporate and private-equity-backed employers see less regulation and lower cost as favorable. The ACGME should be the counterweight, not the accelerant.

The six changes that hit EM hardest

  • Core faculty ratio (2.25) — Caps what any specialty may require at 1 core faculty : 5 residents. EM is 1:3. For USF EM that is 13 required core faculty down to 8.
  • Program director qualifications (2.11–2.13) — Three-year experience minimum replaced by a mentoring plan; non-board-eligible PD can be approved by the GMEC; EM-RRC loses authority to require ABEM/AOBEM or add qualifications.
  • Faculty qualifications and supervision (2.22, 2.24) — GMEC pathway for non-board-eligible faculty; RRC authority to require ABMS/AOA deleted; specialties told their supervision rules “should include flexibility” for other-specialty faculty.
  • Patient care, procedures, medical knowledge (4.4–4.6) — “Residents must demonstrate” becomes “programs must provide instruction in and assess”; RRC authority to specify EM competencies deleted.
  • Scholarship (4.18–4.19) — Faculty minimum becomes one activity every three years (one lecture qualifies); RRC can no longer require peer-reviewed publication or specify resident scholarship.
  • Work hours (6.29–6.39) — 80-hour ceiling is fine; the problems are deleting the 8-hours-off turnaround rule (6.30) and deleting RRC authority to keep EM’s 60-hour week, 12-hour shifts, and non-averaged day off.

How the comment form works

  • Open the form in a normal (not private) browser window. You can close the browser and come back later on the same device; progress is saved.
  • Pages 1–3 are demographics (name, role, institution, specialty). Page 4 asks whether you support everything with no comments or want to continue. Choose CONTINUE.
  • The rest of the form is one page per section of the requirements (about 35 pages). Each page shows the tracked-changes text, then a grid with one row per requirement number and four checkboxes: Support as proposed / Support with additional modifications / Do not support / I wish to include a comment.
  • Nothing is required. You can leave any row blank. Checking “I wish to include a comment” does not open a box on the same page. The comment boxes for the rows you checked (“Comments on 2.8.” etc., 1,600-character limit) appear on the next page after you click Next page. That is where the comment text below goes; use the Copy button next to each comment.
  • Click Next page to move on. Clicking Next too quickly can trigger a “page is out of date” dialog; choose Refresh and re-enter that page. There is no separate Submit button: Next page on the last comment page (6.39.a) submits the survey and shows a thank-you page. There is no confirmation email.
  • Following this guide takes 20–30 minutes. Pages marked PRIORITY are the ones to spend time on; the rest can be left blank or marked Support.
  • Identical pasted comments from many people are weighted as one. Keep the position and the selection, but change a sentence or add one of your own (“As a PGY-2…”, “As core faculty who runs our ultrasound curriculum…”). The ACGME has said it will not reply to individual comments.

Physician faculty who supervise and teach EM residents must be board certified in Emergency Medicine (ABEM/AOBEM). 2.25 DDo not support

This is the most damaging change in the proposal for Emergency Medicine. EM currently requires one core faculty member for every three residents. Capping what a Review Committee may require at 1:5 would cut our program’s required core faculty from 13 to 8. Core faculty status is how an academic department gives protected time to physicians who want to build careers in education and scholarship; with mentorship and career development, those faculty grow into the academicians who write the papers, run the trials, lead quality work, and train the next generation. Over the past decade, GME expansion in EM has been dominated by programs outside academic institutions, with smaller academic faculty groups that often support only the required FTE. A 1:5 ceiling will reduce dedicated faculty for didactics, mentorship, and assessment; reduce the number of emergency physicians who develop as academicians; and accelerate divestment from academic Emergency Medicine. The stated rationale — reducing variability across specialties and lowering barriers for small programs — is administrative, not educational. The Review Committee must retain the authority to set the ratio the specialty needs. If a ceiling is retained at all, it must not override a specialty’s existing, more rigorous standard.
2.25.a MSupport with additional modifications

Retain and strengthen the Review Committee’s authority to specify dedicated, protected non-clinical time for core faculty. Without protected time the core faculty designation is a label, not a resource for residents.

6.29 MSupport with additional modifications

An 80-hour ceiling averaged over four weeks is acceptable as a universal maximum, and flexibility at the block level has real value for scheduling. No EM program schedules residents near that ceiling; typical scheduled ED hours are closer to 45 per week. However, the Review Committee must retain the ability to set specialty-specific limits below the ceiling. I am commenting as [role] in Emergency Medicine at the University of South Florida Morsani College of Medicine and Tampa General Hospital. I support the goal of a simpler, less prescriptive set of Common Program Requirements, and I support the new ultimate board-certification standard. I do not support the parts of this proposal that lower specialty standards and remove Review Committee authority to restore them: the 1:5 core faculty ceiling (2.25), the GMEC pathways and loss of Review Committee authority over program director and faculty certification (2.12, 2.24), the cross-specialty supervision language (2.22), the deletion of specialty authority over patient care, procedural skills, and medical knowledge (4.4–4.6), the scholarship minimum that a single lecture can satisfy (4.18), and the deletion of Review Committee authority over work hours (6.29–6.39). Over the last decade, rapid expansion of Emergency Medicine programs outside academic institutions has already created a heterogeneous training environment in which graduates of accredited programs do not have equal ability. These changes would widen that gap and produce a more tiered distribution of programs, because only departments with resources will choose to exceed the floor. The ACGME’s role should be to (1) ensure standards across programs that produce highly trained physicians for our communities, (2) allow each specialty to adapt and adopt the requirements that fit its field, and (3) act as a counterweight to corporate and private-equity influence in GME, which sees lower cost and less regulation as favorable. Protecting the training of residents and the future of academic medicine is the point of accreditation.
